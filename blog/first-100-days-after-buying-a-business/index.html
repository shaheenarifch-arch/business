First 100 Days After Buying a Business: A Practical Transition Plan

Quick answer

In your first 100 days after buying a business, your job is to keep what you paid for before you try to grow it. Spend days 1 to 30 stabilizing (cash, payroll, access, staff and key customers), days 31 to 60 learning how the business really runs alongside the seller, and days 61 to 100 making a small number of deliberate improvements backed by a weekly scorecard. Change as little as possible in the first month, communicate more than feels necessary, and keep a written log of everything you learn.

Why the first 100 days after an acquisition matter so much

Closing day feels like the finish line, but it is really the start of the hardest part. Everything you paid for, the customers, the staff, the supplier terms, the reputation and the cash flow, now depends on how you handle the transition.

A business that changes hands is fragile for a while. Employees wonder whether their jobs are safe, customers wonder whether service will slip, and suppliers wonder whether the new owner will pay on time. None of that shows up on the financial statements you reviewed.

A 100-day plan gives that period structure. It is not a magic number; it is simply long enough to learn the business properly and short enough to keep you focused. The goal is to protect the cash flow you bought, understand the business well enough to make good decisions, and then start improving it on purpose rather than by accident.

Before day 1: the closing-week handover checklist

The smoothest first 100 days usually start before closing. Use the final week to make sure nothing important is left hanging. Your purchase agreement and your attorney's closing checklist come first; this list covers the practical operating items that are easy to forget.

  • A written transition plan with the seller: what they will do, for how long, and how you will reach them (matching whatever the purchase agreement says)
  • A list of every bank account, merchant account and payment processor, and a plan for changing signers or opening new accounts
  • A complete list of logins: email, website, domain registrar, accounting software, point of sale, payroll, social media, supplier portals and phone system
  • Confirmation of which contracts, leases and licenses are being assigned, which need consent, and which must be reissued in your name
  • Insurance in force from the closing date, in the correct entity name
  • Payroll set up for the first pay date after closing, so nobody misses a paycheck
  • A short, agreed announcement for employees, and a separate one for key customers and suppliers
  • Your own written list of the ten things you most need to learn in the first month

Many of these items connect back to your due diligence. If anything on the due diligence checklist was left open, decide before closing who owns it and by when it will be resolved. For the legal and paperwork side of the closing itself, see our guide to closing on a business purchase.

Days 1 to 30: stabilize the business

The first month has one theme: nothing breaks. You are not trying to impress anyone with bold changes. You are trying to make sure that employees get paid, customers get served, bills get paid and the seller's knowledge starts flowing to you.

Meet the team before you change anything

Your employees know things that are not written down anywhere: which customers need special handling, which supplier is reliable, which machine needs a particular trick. They are also the people most likely to leave if the change of ownership feels threatening.

Hold a short all-hands meeting on day one, ideally with the seller present. Say who you are, why you bought the business, and what will not change in the near term. Be honest about what you do not know yet. Then meet each person individually during the first two weeks. Ask simple questions and listen more than you talk:

  • What does a normal day look like for you?
  • What gets in the way of doing your job well?
  • What should I definitely not change?
  • If you owned the business, what would you fix first?
  • Who should I talk to about the things you do not know?

Write the answers in a single learning log, the running document where you will also record processes, contacts, recurring problems and questions for the seller. Avoid promising raises or new equipment in these first conversations; you do not yet know what the business can afford.

Take control of the money

Cash is the area where a transition can go wrong fastest. In the first days, confirm exactly where money comes in and where it goes out. Check that customer payments are landing in the right account, especially if card processing, online payments or automatic transfers were set up under the seller's name or entity.

How this works depends on your deal structure. In an asset purchase, your new entity typically needs its own bank accounts, its own merchant accounts and its own payroll registrations. In a stock purchase, the existing company usually keeps its accounts, and the work is mainly about changing who can sign and approve payments. Your accountant and attorney should confirm what applies to you.

Set up a simple weekly cash check: what came in, what went out, and what is due in the next two weeks.

Lock down access to accounts and systems

Small businesses often run on accounts that were opened years ago with someone's personal email address. That is a real risk after a sale. Make an inventory of every system the business uses and move ownership to accounts you control. Typical items include:

  • The domain name and website hosting, which are easy to lose if they renew on the seller's personal card
  • Business email and the main phone number
  • Accounting, payroll and point-of-sale software
  • Online listings, review profiles and social media pages
  • Supplier portals, marketplace seller accounts and shipping accounts
  • Alarm codes, physical keys and safe combinations

Change passwords, turn on two-factor authentication where available, and agree in writing how long the seller keeps any access during the transition.

Talk to your most important customers and suppliers

If a handful of customers or suppliers matter a lot to the business, contact them personally in the first weeks, ideally with an introduction from the seller. Keep the message simple: the business has a new owner, the people they deal with are staying, and service will continue. Then ask what they value and what they would like to see improved.

These calls reduce the chance that a key customer quietly drifts away during the uncertainty, and they show whether the relationships you saw in due diligence are as strong as they looked. If something worries you, compare it with our red flags checklist and talk to your advisers.

Days 31 to 60: learn how the business really works

By the second month, the immediate risks should be under control. Now the work shifts from protecting the business to understanding it. This is also when the seller's transition support is most valuable, so plan to use it well.

Work every role, or at least watch it closely

Spend time doing or shadowing each key job: opening and closing, taking orders, handling complaints, purchasing, invoicing and collections. You will learn more in a day of doing than in a week of reading reports, and staff notice whether a new owner is willing to learn the work.

As you go, document the main processes in plain steps. Simple checklists for opening, closing, ordering and month-end make the business less dependent on any single person, including you.

Use the seller's transition period on purpose

Most purchase agreements include some form of seller training or transition support. The length and terms vary from deal to deal, so read yours carefully. Whatever you have, do not waste it on casual chats. Set a weekly agenda with the seller and work through topics in order:

  1. Customers: the history of each major account, pricing arrangements, and any informal promises the seller made.
  2. Suppliers: who to call, what terms you have, and where there may be room to renegotiate.
  3. Seasonality: what the calendar of the business looks like, and when cash gets tight.
  4. Recurring problems: the equipment, staff or customer issues that come back every year.
  5. Unwritten rules: anything the seller does from memory that nobody else knows how to do.

Record what you learn in your log. If the seller is also your lender through a seller note, keep the relationship professional and make payments exactly as agreed; trust between you and the seller is an asset during the transition. Our guide to SBA and seller financing explains how these arrangements are usually structured.

Compare reality with your due diligence and your model

Before closing, you built a picture of the business from tax returns, financial statements and conversations. Now you can test that picture against what actually happens week to week. Look at revenue by customer and product, gross margin, labor costs, and the owner add-backs you accepted when you valued the business.

If you used a seller's discretionary earnings (SDE) figure, check whether the add-backs hold up now that you are running the business. Some expenses the seller treated as one-off may turn out to be real operating costs. Our guide on valuing a business with SDE and EBITDA explains add-backs. The aim is an accurate baseline for planning, not second-guessing the deal.

Days 61 to 100: make the first deliberate improvements

By now you know the people, the customers, the numbers and the weak spots. Start making changes, carefully and visibly.

Pick a few quick wins, not a dozen projects

Go back to your learning log and your employee interviews. Look for improvements that are low risk, cheap, and solve a problem the staff already complained about. Fixing a broken process or replacing a tool that everyone hates builds goodwill quickly. Limit yourself to a small number of changes at a time so you can tell what worked.

Good early candidates include clearer scheduling, tighter follow-up on unpaid invoices, and fixing the website basics customers see first. Bigger moves, such as raising prices, changing suppliers or restructuring roles, usually deserve more time and more data.

Build a simple weekly scorecard

Choose a short list of numbers that show whether the business is healthy, and review them every week. The right measures depend on the business, but a scorecard might include:

  • Cash in the bank and cash expected in and out over the next few weeks
  • Sales for the week compared with the same week last year, if records allow
  • Gross margin or food, labor and material costs as a share of sales
  • Number of new customers, orders or jobs booked
  • Unpaid customer invoices that are past due
  • One quality or customer satisfaction signal, such as complaints, returns or reviews

Sharing a version with your team signals that decisions will be based on facts.

Write the plan for the rest of year one

Around day 90, step back and write a one-page plan for the next nine months. List the few priorities that matter most, the budget each needs, who owns it, and how you will know it worked. Include the risks you discovered and what you will do about them. Review the plan with your accountant, and with your lender if your loan agreement asks for updates.

Money matters to watch in the first 100 days

Cash can feel tight in the first months: loan payments start and one-off transition costs add up. Watch these areas closely.

Working capital

Your purchase agreement may include a working capital target or adjustment, or it may not. Either way, check how much cash the business needs to cover payroll, rent, inventory and supplier bills between the time it pays out and the time customers pay in. If you financed the deal, your lender may have built some working capital into the loan; know exactly how much is available and what it can be used for.

Debt service and loan conditions

Put every loan payment date into your calendar, including any seller note. Read the loan agreement for reporting obligations, such as financial statements or tax returns the lender expects to receive, and for any conditions you must keep meeting.

Earn-outs and holdbacks

If part of the price depends on future performance or was held back, track the figures exactly as the agreement defines them and agree early with the seller how they will be reported.

Taxes and bookkeeping

Start your books cleanly on the closing date, with the purchase recorded the way your purchase agreement and accountant set out, and confirm sales tax and payroll tax filing dates under your ownership.

Legal and admin tasks that cannot wait

Paperwork is rarely exciting, but missed filings and lapsed permits can cause real trouble. Work through these with your attorney and accountant during the first month:

  • Business licenses and permits: some transfer, some must be reissued in the new owner's name. Health permits, liquor licenses and certain professional licenses are common examples that need attention.
  • Lease: confirm the assignment or new lease is signed, and note renewal dates and notice periods.
  • Contracts: confirm which customer, supplier and equipment contracts were assigned and whether any needed consent.
  • Insurance: review coverage limits and named insureds now that you know the business better.
  • Employees: complete any new-hire paperwork your entity needs, and check benefits plans continue without gaps.
  • Entity compliance: note your entity's annual report deadline and keep your registered agent information current.

If you bought through a new LLC, our guide on whether you need a registered agent explains the role. Buyers who formed their entity through a formation service should check which compliance reminders and registered agent coverage their package includes; our Bizee review covers one option, and you can see the service directly at Bizee. Note any renewal dates so nothing lapses.

Closing near year end? What changes for a Q1 handover

Deals that close in late November or December put the first weeks of ownership right on top of year-end work. That is manageable, but it needs planning:

  • Year-end payroll and tax forms: agree in writing who prepares year-end payroll forms and information returns for the part of the year before closing, and who prepares them after.
  • Inventory counts: if the business counts inventory at year end, coordinate the count with any closing inventory figure in your agreement.
  • Holiday trading: in retail, hospitality and gift-related businesses, the busiest weeks of the year may land in your first month. Lean on existing staff and the seller, and leave changes until after the rush.
  • Annual renewals: licenses, insurance policies, software subscriptions and domain names often renew in January. List them before closing.

If you are still deciding when to close, discuss timing with your accountant.

Common mistakes new owners make in the first 100 days

  • Changing too much, too fast. New logos, new software and new rules in the first month can unsettle staff and customers just when they need reassurance.
  • Ignoring the seller's knowledge. The transition period is limited. Use it with a plan.
  • Losing control of accounts. Domains, payment processors and online profiles left in the seller's name can cause trouble months later.
  • Not watching cash weekly. Profit on paper does not pay this week's payroll.
  • Doing everything alone. Your accountant, attorney, lender and a trusted mentor can help you avoid expensive first-year errors.

How to judge whether your first 100 days went well

A successful transition does not need dramatic growth. At day 100, ask yourself:

  • Did the key employees stay, and do they trust the new direction?
  • Are the main customers still buying?
  • Is cash flow close to what you expected, and do you understand any differences?
  • Can you run the business without calling the seller?
  • Are your main processes written down?
  • Do you have a short, realistic plan for the rest of the year?

If most answers are yes, you have done the hard part. If not, you now know where to focus.

FAQ

What should I do first after buying a business?

Make sure payroll, cash and payments work under your ownership, take control of the business's accounts and logins, and meet every employee. Hold off on major changes until you understand how the business runs.

Should I make big changes right after buying a business?

Usually not in the first month. Most new owners do better by stabilizing first, learning for a few weeks, and then making a small number of planned changes backed by data and staff input.

How long does the seller stay after the sale?

It depends on the purchase agreement. Many deals include a seller training or transition period, but the length and terms vary, so read your agreement and plan to use that time with a clear agenda.

How do I tell employees about the new ownership?

Tell them early, ideally on day one and with the seller present. Explain who you are, what will not change in the near term, and how you plan to learn the business. Then meet each person individually.

Do business licenses transfer to a new owner?

Some do and some do not. Certain licenses and permits must be reissued in the new owner's name, and some need approval before closing. Check each license with the issuing agency and your attorney.

What numbers should a new owner track every week?

A short list is enough: cash in the bank and expected cash flow, weekly sales, gross margin or key costs, new orders or customers, overdue invoices, and one quality or customer satisfaction measure.
