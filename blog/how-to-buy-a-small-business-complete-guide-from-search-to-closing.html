Quick answer

How to buy a small business: complete guide from search to closing involves six broad stages: define your criteria, search listings or brokers, value the target, arrange financing, complete due diligence, and close with signed legal documents. Each stage builds on the last, and skipping steps to save time is one of the most common causes of post-purchase disputes or failed deals.

  • Budget, including purchase price plus working capital and advisory fees
  • Industry familiarity or willingness to learn operations quickly
  • Financing readiness, such as SBA pre-qualification or available cash
  • Time available for several months of search and negotiation
  • Risk tolerance for owner-dependent or seasonal businesses
  • Access to an attorney and accountant for closing documents

Key takeaways

  • Valuation is typically built around seller's discretionary earnings or EBITDA multiples rather than annual revenue alone, so two businesses with similar sales can have very different asking prices.
  • Seller financing and SBA-backed loans are commonly used to reduce the cash a buyer needs at closing, but lenders usually still require a meaningful down payment.
  • A signed letter of intent sets expected terms but is generally non-binding on price; due diligence findings can still lead to renegotiation or withdrawal.
  • Closing documents, licenses, and lease assignments often take longer to finalize than the financial negotiation itself.
  • Working with a broker, attorney, and accountant adds cost but is widely used to reduce legal and financial exposure during a purchase.

Definitions

Letter of Intent (LOI)

A letter of intent is a preliminary written agreement outlining the proposed price, structure, and timeline for a deal. It signals serious interest and often grants a period of exclusivity, but it generally is not a binding commitment to complete the purchase.

Due diligence

Due diligence is the structured review of a target business's financial records, contracts, licenses, and operations before closing. It is designed to confirm that what was represented in the listing and negotiations matches reality.

Seller's Discretionary Earnings (SDE)

SDE is a common earnings measure for small businesses that adds back the owner's salary, discretionary expenses, and non-cash items to net profit. It is frequently used as the base figure for applying a valuation multiple.

Core concepts

How buyers typically find businesses for sale

Buyers generally use a mix of online marketplaces, business brokers, and direct outreach to owners in a target industry. Marketplace listings vary widely in detail, so initial inquiries usually require signing a non-disclosure agreement before financial information is shared.

How value and price are determined

Valuation combines a financial baseline, such as SDE or EBITDA, with a multiple that reflects industry norms, growth trends, customer concentration, and owner dependency. Asking price and final negotiated price can differ once due diligence surfaces new information.

How deals get financed

Buyers commonly combine personal cash, SBA loans, and seller financing to fund a purchase, with the mix depending on lender requirements and the seller's willingness to carry part of the note. Financing terms affect both the structure of the offer and the timeline to close.

How risk is allocated between buyer and seller

Purchase agreements allocate risk through representations, warranties, indemnities, and the choice between an asset sale or a stock sale. Asset sales are more common for small businesses because they generally let buyers avoid inheriting unknown liabilities tied to the prior entity.

What closing actually involves

Closing brings together the final purchase agreement, lender documents, lease assignment or new lease, license transfers, and allocation of the purchase price for tax purposes. Multiple parties, including attorneys, lenders, and sometimes a landlord, need to sign off before funds are released.

Common mistakes

  • Skipping a formal valuation. Relying only on the seller's asking price can lead to overpaying; request an independent read on SDE or EBITDA multiples before negotiating.
  • Underestimating working capital needs. Buyers who spend all available cash on the purchase price can struggle with payroll or inventory in the first months; budget for a reserve beyond the acquisition cost.
  • Treating the LOI as final. Assuming a signed letter of intent guarantees the deal can lead to premature decisions like resigning a job; treat it as a framework subject to due diligence.
  • Rushing or skipping due diligence. Incomplete review of contracts, licenses, and financial statements can hide liabilities; follow a structured due diligence checklist rather than relying on seller summaries alone.
  • Ignoring owner dependency. A business that relies heavily on the current owner's relationships or skills can lose value quickly after the sale; ask how transferable customer and supplier relationships are.
  • Underestimating the timeline. Buyers who expect to close in weeks are often surprised by financing and legal delays; review a realistic timeline for buying a business before setting expectations.
  • Not budgeting for professional fees. Broker commissions, legal fees, and lender costs add to the total spend; compare broker fees versus direct buying before deciding on an approach.
  • Missing red flags in financial statements. Inconsistent records or unexplained adjustments can signal deeper problems; review a red flags checklist alongside your accountant's review.

Limitations and checks

Listing details, financial figures, and asking prices change over time, so current information should always be confirmed directly on the retailer or broker page rather than assumed from earlier research. Financing terms, including SBA eligibility and seller financing willingness, vary by lender and by deal, and this guide cannot predict what any specific lender will approve.

No hands-on evaluation of any listed business took place in preparing this guide; all business descriptions reflect what the retailer or broker has published. Legal structures, licensing requirements, and tax treatment of a purchase also vary by state and industry, so a licensed attorney and accountant should review any specific deal before signing. Bottom line

Buying a small business works best as a disciplined process rather than a single transaction: define your criteria, verify value against earnings rather than the asking price alone, line up financing early, and treat due diligence as non-negotiable before closing. There is no single best business type for every buyer; the right fit depends on your budget, risk tolerance, and willingness to be operationally involved. The practical next step is to pick a target industry and price range, then start screening listings using a structured framework rather than reacting to the first opportunity that appears.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it typically take to buy a small business?

Most acquisitions take several months from initial search to closing, with financing and due diligence usually accounting for the largest portion of that time. Complex deals involving SBA loans or lease assignments can extend the timeline further, so buyers should plan for delays rather than assume a fast close.

Do I need a business broker to buy a small business?

No, buying directly from an owner is possible, but brokers can simplify sourcing, negotiation, and paperwork in exchange for a fee. Whether a broker is worth the cost depends on your experience with acquisitions and how much time you can dedicate to the search yourself.

What is the difference between an asset sale and a stock sale?

An asset sale transfers specific business assets and liabilities chosen in the agreement, while a stock sale transfers ownership of the entire legal entity, including unknown liabilities. Small business buyers more commonly pursue asset sales because they generally limit exposure to past obligations of the seller's company.

How much cash do I need to buy a small business?

The amount varies by deal size, financing structure, and lender requirements, but buyers using SBA loans or seller financing typically still need a meaningful down payment plus reserves for working capital. There is no fixed percentage that applies to every transaction.

What should due diligence cover?

Due diligence should cover financial statements, tax filings, contracts, leases, licenses, employee records, and any pending legal issues tied to the business. A structured checklist helps ensure nothing is overlooked before the purchase agreement is finalized.

Is seller financing common for small business purchases?

Yes, many small business sales include some seller financing, where the owner carries part of the purchase price as a note repaid over time. Terms vary by deal and depend on the seller's willingness and the buyer's negotiated agreement.

Can I back out after signing a letter of intent?

Generally yes, because most letters of intent are non-binding on the final price and terms, though some provisions like confidentiality or exclusivity periods can be binding. Review the specific language of any LOI with an attorney before signing or walking away.

What happens if due diligence uncovers a problem?

Buyers typically renegotiate price or terms, request specific indemnities, or in some cases withdraw from the deal entirely. The appropriate response depends on the severity of the issue and whether it was disclosed earlier in the process.
