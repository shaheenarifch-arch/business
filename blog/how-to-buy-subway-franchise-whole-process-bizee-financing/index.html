How to Buy a Subway Franchise Resale: The Whole Process, Step by Step

TL;DR

Buying an existing Subway takes about 60 to 120 days and runs in 10 steps: budget and lender pre-qualification, NDA, forming your LLC, letter of intent, due diligence, Subway's transfer approval, financing, lease assignment, licenses and insurance, then closing and training. Bizee sets up the company that buys the store. An SBA 7(a) loan, often combined with a seller note, pays for most of it. A CPA, a franchise attorney, an insurance agent and the landlord handle the rest. Below we walk through each step using the $99,000 Shelby Township Subway as the example.

2. The 10-step sale process

Step 1: Set your budget and get pre-qualified

Before you contact the seller, know what you can afford. Add up your cash, retirement accounts and home equity, then talk to an SBA lender. A pre-qualification letter shows the seller you are a serious buyer. For this store, plan on about $12,500 of your own money plus reserves (see the financing example below).

Step 2: Contact the seller and sign the NDA

The seller requires a signed non-disclosure agreement (NDA) before sharing the store name, address and financials. Email raysvantagepoint@gmail.com. Do not contact the store or its employees. That is a firm condition of this sale, and it protects the business you are buying.

Step 3: Form your buying company with Bizee

Most buyers buy a franchise through an LLC rather than in their own name. Your lender, Subway and the landlord will all want to know which company is buying. How Bizee helps below covers what to order and why to do it now.

Step 4: Sign a letter of intent (LOI)

The LOI sets out the price ($99,000 asking), what is included (equipment, inventory, the walk-in cooler, freezer, oven and POS), and the conditions your purchase depends on:

  • Getting your financing
  • Subway approving the transfer to you
  • The landlord consenting to assign the lease
  • Due diligence confirming the seller's numbers
  • Whether there will be a seller note, and its terms

Have your attorney review it before you sign. The LOI is usually non-binding on price but sets the deal's shape.

Step 5: Do due diligence (with your CPA)

The seller reports $295,000 in annual sales and a $45,000 owner benefit. Your job is to prove both. Ask for:

  • 3 years of business tax returns and profit & loss statements
  • 12 to 24 months of POS sales reports (they should match the deposits in the bank statements)
  • Royalty and marketing-fund statements paid to Subway (8% + 4.5% of sales should line up with reported sales)
  • Payroll records for the 1 full-time and 3 part-time staff
  • The lease, CAM charges ($2,664/month in total) and any landlord notices
  • A list of equipment with the age and condition of each item
  • Health inspection reports and any Subway operations reviews
  • Confirmation of the remaining term on the franchise agreement (about 15 years)

Your CPA should also check whether the seller owes any sales, payroll or other taxes. In Michigan a buyer can become liable for a seller's unpaid state taxes, so ask your CPA or attorney about requesting a tax clearance from the Michigan Department of Treasury. Until that comes back, hold back part of the price in escrow. For a full list, use our 28-point due diligence checklist and red flags checklist.

Step 6: Get Subway's transfer approval

You cannot buy a franchise without the franchisor's consent. Subway's transfer process requires approval of the buyer, a financial qualification, training, and payment of a transfer fee. Expect to submit an application with your personal financial statement, and possibly to interview. Subway also checks that the seller is up to date on royalties. Approval and the training schedule are often what decides how fast you can close. Start this right after the LOI, in parallel with your loan. Your SBA lender will not close until Subway has approved you.

Step 7: Finalize your financing

Your lender underwrites the loan using the verified numbers from Step 5. At this size, the usual approach is an SBA 7(a) loan, often with a small seller note. See How financing helps below for the structure, rules and a worked example.

Step 8: Assign the lease (and try to extend it)

The current lease runs to March 2028, plus one 5-year option. The landlord must consent to the assignment. Lenders usually want the lease, including options, to run about as long as the loan. A 10-year loan starting in 2026 would end around 2036, while the lease with its option ends around 2033. Ask the landlord for an extension or an extra option now, while you have leverage as the incoming tenant. Your attorney should review any personal guarantee the landlord asks for.

Step 9: Licenses, insurance, payroll and bank account

  • Food service license: a license does not simply carry over to a new owner. Apply with the Macomb County Health Department in your company's name before closing.
  • Sales tax and payroll: register your LLC with the Michigan Department of Treasury for sales and withholding tax, and set up payroll. Your CPA can do both.
  • Insurance: general liability, property, and workers' compensation for the staff. Check Subway's and the landlord's minimum coverage and the additional-insured wording they require.
  • Business bank account: open it using your EIN and LLC documents. The lender will fund through it.

Step 10: Close, then train

At closing you sign the asset purchase agreement, the bill of sale, the lease assignment and Subway's transfer documents. The lender funds, the seller is paid (minus any escrow holdback), and inventory is counted and added to the price as agreed. After that, the seller gives 2 weeks of on-site training, and you take over a store that is open 9am to 11pm with its existing team.

4. How financing helps (with a worked example)

Very few buyers pay cash. Financing lets you keep reserves for payroll and surprises, and the lender's underwriting gives you a second check on the seller's numbers.

Option A: SBA 7(a) loan (most common)

The U.S. Small Business Administration guarantees part of a bank's loan, so the bank can lend on a business purchase with a 10-year term. The rules that matter for this deal:

  • 10% equity injection. For a complete change of ownership, the SBA requires at least 10% of the total project cost from the buyer. That includes closing costs and working capital, not just the price.
  • Seller note on full standby. A seller note can count for up to half of that 10%. To count, it must be on full standby, meaning no principal or interest payments for the life of the SBA loan.
  • Rate caps. For a loan between $50,001 and $250,000, the SBA caps variable rates at prime plus 6%. Lenders often quote less.
  • Franchise eligibility. Ask your lender to confirm Subway's listing in the SBA Franchise Directory. The lender will also need Subway's transfer approval before closing.

One practical point: some banks do not like very small SBA loans. Ask local community banks, credit unions and SBA-focused lenders, and compare at least 2 or 3 quotes.

Option B: Seller financing (outside the SBA)

Some sellers carry part of the price as a note paid over 3 to 5 years. That's flexible and fast, but if you also use an SBA loan, any payments on that note count against your debt-service calculation, and only a note on full standby counts as equity. Ask the seller early whether they will carry a note at all.

Option C: Other sources

  • Home equity line of credit (HELOC): often cheaper, but it puts your home at risk. Most lenders will not let borrowed money count as your equity injection.
  • 401(k) rollover (ROBS): lets you invest retirement money in your own business without early-withdrawal penalties when it is set up correctly. It is complex and IRS-sensitive, so use a specialist and a CPA.
  • Partner or family investor: under SBA rules, a minority investor's equity is treated much like a seller note: it can count toward the 10%, within the same cap.

6. Closing-day checklist

  • Subway's written transfer approval and signed franchise documents
  • SBA loan approval, closing documents, and your equity injection deposited
  • Signed landlord consent to the lease assignment (and any extension)
  • Food service license in your company's name
  • Insurance certificates naming Subway, the landlord and the lender as required
  • Asset purchase agreement, bill of sale, equipment list, non-compete
  • Escrow or holdback for taxes and any unresolved items
  • Inventory count and POS, payroll and bank access transferred
  • Training dates confirmed with the seller and Subway

7. FAQ

Do I need an LLC to buy a Subway?

Not legally, but most buyers use one, and lenders and franchisors are used to it. An LLC formed through a service like Bizee, with an EIN and operating agreement, keeps the business separate from your personal finances. You will usually still sign some personal guarantees.

How much cash do I need to buy this $99,000 Subway?

With an SBA loan, plan on at least 10% of total project cost, about $12,500 in our example. Up to half of that can come from a seller note on full standby. Keep extra reserves on top.

Can Subway reject me as a buyer?

Yes. Every franchise transfer needs the franchisor's approval, which usually involves a financial qualification, an application and training. Start the application right after your LOI.

How long does the whole purchase take?

Usually 60 to 120 days, depending on Subway's approval and training schedule, the loan and the landlord's consent.
